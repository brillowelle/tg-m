01

What is Cataract?

A cataract is a clouding of the eye’s natural lens, the clear part behind the iris that focuses light onto the retina.

As we age, proteins in the lens slowly clump together and the lens becomes cloudy and yellowish. Light can no longer pass through clearly, so vision gradually becomes blurred, dim and faded, as if looking through a frosted window.

Cataracts are very common after the age of 60, but they can also be linked to diabetes, eye injury, long-term steroid use or strong sunlight exposure. They cannot be cleared with glasses or medicines, but they can be treated very effectively with surgery.

02

Symptoms

Cataracts usually develop slowly, so changes can be easy to miss at first. Common signs include:

  • Cloudy or blurry vision

    Everything looks hazy, as if through a misty glass.

  • Faded colours

    Colours look dull, yellowed or less bright than before.

  • Glare and halos

    Sunlight, lamps and headlights feel too bright or show rings.

  • Poor night vision

    Driving or reading in low light becomes difficult.

  • Frequent prescription changes

    New glasses help for a short time, then vision drops again.

  • Double vision in one eye

    Seeing a ghost image when looking with one eye only.

03

When is Surgery Needed?

In the early stages, stronger glasses and better lighting may be enough. Surgery is advised when the cataract starts to affect your daily life or the health of your eye.

You don’t need to wait for a cataract to “ripen”. Modern surgery works well at any stage, and waiting too long can make the operation more complex.

Surgery is usually recommended when

  • Blurred vision makes reading, driving or work difficult
  • Glasses no longer give you clear enough vision
  • Glare at night makes driving unsafe
  • The cataract stops your doctor from checking the retina, for example in diabetes
  • The cataract is causing other problems, such as raised eye pressure

05

Procedure

  1. 1

    Consultation & tests

    A full eye examination and painless scans to measure your eye and choose the lens power.

  2. 2

    Preparing on the day

    Drops widen your pupil and numb the eye. You stay awake and comfortable.

  3. 3

    The surgery

    Through a tiny opening, the cloudy lens is gently removed and a clear artificial lens is placed. It usually takes 15–30 minutes.

  4. 4

    Going home

    After a short rest you go home the same day with a protective shield and eye drops.

06

Recovery

  1. First day

    Vision may be blurry and the eye may feel gritty. Rest and wear the eye shield.

  2. First week

    Vision usually improves within a few days. Use your eye drops exactly as prescribed.

  3. Weeks 2–4

    Most daily activities return to normal. Your eye continues to settle.

  4. After about a month

    The eye has healed and you can be checked for new glasses if needed.

Do

  • Use your eye drops on time
  • Wear the shield while sleeping
  • Wear sunglasses outdoors
  • Keep your follow-up appointments

Avoid

  • Rubbing or pressing the eye
  • Getting water or soap in the eye
  • Swimming and dusty places
  • Heavy lifting for the first weeks
